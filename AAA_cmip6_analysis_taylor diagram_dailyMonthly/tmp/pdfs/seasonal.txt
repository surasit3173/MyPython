ABSTRACT  
Rainfall variability in Thailand is a critical factor for agriculture, water resources, and  disaster risk 
management. The seasonal and regional impacts of the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), a significant contributor 
to interannual variability, remain inadequately characterized at the national scale. This study validates ERA5 monthly 
precipitation data against observations from Thai Meteorological Department (TMD)  stations across four 
representative regions and examines ENSO -related rainfall  anomalies from 1995 to 2024. The validation results 
indicate that ERA5 effectively  reproduces seasonal cycles and interannual variability, with coefficients of  
determination ranging from 0.80 to 0.87 for annual rainfall across all stations. Overall,  ERA5 demonstrates strong 
performance in tropical regions, underscoring its suitability  for climate analysis. The climato logical results reveal 
significant seasonal differences,  with the rainy season contributing the majority of annual rainfall. ENSO exerts  
significant influence on Thai rainfall patterns. El Niño events are associated with  reduced precipitation, particularly  
during summer and monsoon seasons, whereas La Niña events enhance rainfall, especially over the northeastern and 
central regions. Although winter impacts are less pronounced, they remain evident in the southern  peninsula. These

HIGHLIGHTS 
• ERA5 precipitation shows strong agreement with TMD observations (R² = 0.80 –0.87), confirming its reliability 
for rainfall analysis in Thailand. 
• ENSO significantly modulates rainfall variability: El Niño is associated with reduced rainfall, while La Niña 
enhances precipitation, especially in central and northeastern regions.  
• Seasonal impacts are pronounced, with strongest ENSO influence during summer and monsoon, highlighting 
implications for agriculture and water management. 
 
1. INTRODUCTION  
 Rainfall is a key component of Thailand's climate system. It influences agricultural planting decisions, water 
resources management, and disaster preparedness. As rice is the country’s main crop, its production relies heavily on 
seasonal rainfall, making a gricultural output particularly sensitive to ENSO related rainfall anomalies [1]. This makes 
farming communities highly vulnerable to rainfall variability [2]. Alterations in the timing or amount of precipitation 
can have profound impacts. For example, droughts reduce yields and threaten food security, whereas floods damage 
urban areas and farmland, resulting in long -term social and economic costs. Understanding rainfall variability is 
therefore not only a scientific challenge but also a practical necessity  for advancing sustainable development and 
enhancing climate resilience [3, 4].  
 The El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is a major driver of interannual climate variability, exerting a strong 
influence on the Asian monsoon, particularly in Thailand [5]. El Niño events are typically associated with reduced 
rainfall and heightened drought risk, whereas La Niña events increase rainfall and raise the likelihood of flooding. 
Previous studies [6 -8] have documented these teleconnections; however, most have relie d on station -based 
observations or low-resolution reanalysis data [9-11]. Although these methods yielded important insights, they were 
inadequate for capturing spatial asymmetries and seasonal variability. Recent studies have begun to overcome these 
limitations by employing high resolution reanalysis and model -based assessments. For instance, ENSO has been 
shown to significantly modulates rainfall variability across northeastern Thailand, underscoring the importance of 
spatially explicit analyses [12]. The ENSO–rainfall relationship is inherently dynamic, with variability in strength across 
seasons and potential shifts under changing climate conditions [3, 13 -14]. ENSO is one of the dominant modes 
influencing Thailand's year to year rainfall variability, alt hough other climate factors also play a role. Additionally, 
these climate modes significantly modulate regional ocean dynamics, such as the South Java Current (SJC), which 
exhibits varying degrees of sensitivity to IOD and ENSO across different seasons [15 ,16].  Similar findings were 
reported by [17], who demonstrated that concurrent ENSO -IOD events can modify the intensity and spatial 
distribution of monsoon rainfall across South and Southeast Asia. Previous studies have suggested that the Madden –
Julian Os cillation (MJO) may contribute to sub -seasonal rainfall variability and intra -seasonal rainfall extremes in 
Southeast Asia. Despite notable progress, significant gaps remain. Few studies have validated high -resolution 
reanalysis products such as ERA5 (0.25°) for Thailand, and even fewer have systematically examined ENSO's seasonal 
and regional impacts on rainfall [18,19]. As a result, it remains unclear how effectively reanalysis data represent 
rainfall variability for applications in climate risk assessment and adaptation planning.  
 This study addresses these gaps by validating ERA5 monthly precipitation against long -term records from four 
Thai Meteorological Department (TMD) stations representing the country’s main regions, using a 30 -year dataset 
(1995–2024). In addition, the study further examines ENSO related precipitation anomalies across Thailand’s seasonal 
cycle. This integrated approach offers a comprehensive understanding of the ENSO–rainfall relationship over time and 
space, highlighting regional differences and extreme events such as the 2011 floods and the 2015–2016 drought. The 
findings provide both scientific contributions and practical insights for agriculture, water resource management, and 
disaster risk reduction in Thailand.

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS  
2.1 Study Area  
 Thailand is located in mainland Southeast Asia, extending from 4° to 21°N latitude and 96° to 106°E longitude, 
and is strongly influenced by the Asian monsoon system. During the Southwest Monsoon (May to October), Th ailand 
receives substantial rainfall, particularly along the western coast and eastern shores. In contrast, the Northeast 
Monsoon (November to February) generally brings dry conditions across most of the country, although the southern 
peninsula continues t o experience rainfall due to its coastal location. This monsoon -driven climate regime makes 
Thailand an important setting for examining rainfall variability and its linkages with large -scale climate drivers. 
 For ERA5 precipitation validation, four Thai Me teorological Department (TMD) stations were selected to 
represent Thailand’s major climatic regions: Chiang Mai (18.77°N, 98.97°E) for the northern highlands, Ubon 
Ratchathani (15.24°N, 104.87°E) for the northeastern plateau, Bangkok (13.66°N, 100.61°E) fo r the central plain, and 
Phuket (8.10°N, 98.31°E) for the southern peninsula (Figure 1). These stations were selected because they provide 
long-term, continuous rainfall observations and broad geographical coverage across Thailand’s principal climatic 
zones. The objective of the validation was to assess the capability of ERA5 in reproducing large -scale regional rainfall 
characteristics under different climatic regimes rather than localized precipitation variability. Although individual 
stations may not fully represent subregional heterogeneity, including urban influences and local topographic effects, 
the selected stations provide a practical basis for evaluating ERA5 performance at the national scale. The ERA5 product 
is widely utilized in climate studies d ue to its high spatial resolution, although its precipitation performance requires 
careful regional evaluation, particularly in tropical monsoon regions [20].  Although additional TMD stations are 
available, the present study prioritized representative long -term stations with continuous observations to ensure 
temporal consistency in ERA5 validation.

2.2 Data and Sources 
 This study employed three primary datasets. The ERA5 reanalysis, a state-of-the-art product developed by the 
European Centre for Medium -Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), provided monthly precipitation data. ERA5 offers 
global atmospheric variables at a spatial resolution of 0.25° × 0.25° and was utilized in this study for the period   1995–
2024 [21]. The dataset integrates multiple observational sources, including satellites, ground stations, ships, and 
aircraft, into a sophisticated numerical weather predic tion system, thereby providing a geographically coherent and 
temporally continuous record suitable for climatological analysis. To evaluate the accuracy of rainfall estimates from 
ERA5, monthly rainfall data from ground -based weather stations operated by t he Thai Meteorological Department 
(TMD) were utilized. Four representative stations were selected to capture Thailand’s major climatic regions: Chiang 
Mai in the northern highlands, Ubon Ratchathani in the northeastern plateau, Bangkok in the central plain, and Phuket 
in the southern peninsula. These locations were chosen because they reflect distinct rainfall regimes across the 
country, thereby providing suitable benchmarks for validating and confirming ERA5 estimates.

(+0.5 °C), and the blue dashed line indicates the La Niña threshold (−0.5 °C). Positive anomalies above the red line

 The Oceanic Niño Index (ONI), which measures sea surface temperature anomalies (SSTAs) in the Niño 3.4 
region (5°S–5°N, 120°W–170°W), was employed to identify ENSO phases. Acco rding to NOAA’s definition, an El Niño 
event is characterized by five consecutive overlapping three-month running means of ONI values at or above +0.5 °C, 
while a La Niña event is defined by values at or below −0.5 °C [22]. This threshold -based approach ensures that only 
persistent warm or cool episodes are classified, while short -term fluctuations are excluded. For this study, ONI data 
covering the period from 1995 to 2024 were obtained from NOAA’s Climate Monitoring website (National Centers for 
Environmental Information (NCEI), n.d., https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/monitoring/enso/sst/). The ONI index for 
this period is presented in Figure 2. Red shading indicates warm-phase events (El Niño), whereas blue shading denotes 
cool-phase events (La Niña). Nota ble strong El Niño events occurred in 1997 –1998 and 2015 –2016, while strong La 
Niña events were recorded in 1999–2000 and 2010–2011. These episodes serve as the basis for the composite rainfall 
anomaly analysis discussed in Section 4.2.  ENSO classification  was performed separately for each season using 
seasonal ONI values corresponding to the study periods (summer, rainy, and winter). Years with transitions between 
ENSO phases were classified according to the prevailing seasonal ONI condition rather than an  annual classification, 
thereby minimizing ambiguity associated with mid-year phase changes.

standard seasons, namely summer (February –May), rainy season (June –October), and winter (November –January),

relatively weak signals from background variability and for investigating the broader effects of teleconnections 
[23,24]. 
 To evaluate the statistical significa nce of ENSO -related precipitation anomalies, a one -sample t -test was 
applied to precipitation anomaly fields relative to neutral -year climatology. Rainfall anomalies were calculated by 
subtracting the neutral -year mean from El Niño and La Niña composites. Statistical significance was assessed at the 
95% confidence level (p < 0.05). Significant grid cells are indicated by black stippling in the composite anomaly maps.

4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
4.1 Rainfall Climatology over Thailand 
 The 30 years ERA5 climatology (1995 –2024) reveals that rainfall in Thailand is highly seasonal and exhibits 
substantial spatial variability across the country. This pattern is primarily driven by the Asian monsoon sy stem, while 
geographical and maritime influences also play important roles. Figure 4 presents the spatial distribution of annual 
and seasonal precipitation, and Table 2 summarizes the key statistics for all variables included in the dataset. Most 
areas receiving more than 1,400 – 1,600 mm of annual rainfall are located along the Tenasserim mountain range on 
both the western and eastern coasts. These spatial patterns are consistent with the influence of southwest monsoon 
circulation and regional topographic effects reported in previous studies.  The northern highlands also experience 
substantial annual rainfall, as the region’s complex terrain intensifies convective activity. Conversely, the central plain 
and northeastern plateau are considerably drier, receiv ing only about 1,000 –1,200 mm of rainfall annually, as the 
surrounding mountain ranges create a pronounced rain shadow effect. In contrast, the southern peninsula 
experiences persistently high rainfall throughout the year due to its exposure to both the An daman Sea and the Gulf 
of Thailand. This spatial pattern is consistent with findings from terrestrial analyses [6, 8] and recent satellite-derived 
climatologies [5, 36]. Seasonal variations are pronounced. During winter, most of Thailand receives substanti ally less 
rainfall, typically below 200 mm in total, as the northeast monsoon transports cool, dry continental air masses. 
However, the southern region, particularly along the Gulf of Thailand coast, continues to experience elevated rainfall 
because moistu re is advected from the South China Sea a mechanism consistent with [7], who highlighted the 
influence of northeast monsoon surges in sustaining wet conditions in the far south.  
 From February to May, the climate in Thailand undergoes a transitional period . In the northern and 
northeastern regions, rainfall remains modest (typically below 400 mm) but begins to increase as the southwest 
monsoon approaches. During this time, most precipitation still falls along the coastal areas, while the southern 
peninsula continues to experience higher rainfall throughout the year due to its constant exposure to maritime 
influences. Recent climatological studies [37] have identified similar transitional trends. The rainy season exhibits the 
most pronounced precipitation, wi th totals exceeding 1,200 to 1,600 mm in several regions. This peak occurs when 
the southwest monsoon becomes fully established, transporting abundant moisture from the Andaman Sea and the 
Indian Ocean across mainland Thailand. Heavy rainfall is particular ly common in northern and western Thailand, as 
well as along the eastern seaboard, where orographic uplift further intensifies precipitation. These findings are 
consistent with satellite -based studies [5, 36], which demonstrated how monsoon circulation int eracts with 
orographic features to produce substantial rainfall. During this period, more than 70% of Thailand’s annual 
precipitation occurs, underscoring the monsoon’s critical role in the nation’s hydrological cycle.

 Overall, the climatological analysis reveals a monsoon dominated rainfall regime characterized by a prolonged 
dry season during winter and early summer, followed by an intense rainy season that contributes the majority of 
annual precipitation. By integrating high -resolution reanalysi s data with ground -based observations, the present

the mechanisms monsoonal circulation, orographic lifting, coastal orientation, and ra in-shadow effects that govern 
its variability. The agreement between ERA5 data and ground -based observations, as demonstrated in Section 3, 
reinforces the reliability of ERA5 in capturing Thailand’s spatial and seasonal rainfall patterns. This baseline 
climatological information, derived from ERA5 and validated against station data, provides a robust foundation for 
analyzing interannual variations and assessing the influence of ENSO on rainfall across different seasons. Having 
established the baseline climatology of Thailand’s rainfall, the subsequent analysis explores how ENSO phases modify 
these seasonal and spatial patterns, with particular emphasis on the asymmetric impacts of El Niño and La Niña events. 
 
4.2 ENSO Influence on Rainfall over Thailand 
 ENSO exerts a profound influence on rainfall variability across Thailand, although its magnitude and spatial 
manifestation vary by season. Composite anomaly maps (Figure 5) and domain averaged statistics reveal the 
asymmetric impacts of El Niño and La Niña on precipitation patterns, reinforcing ENSO’s important role in modulating 
interannual hydrological variability in the region. 
 Summer exhibits a strong ENSO influence, with El Niño and La Niña events generating contrasting rainfall 
anomalies. During El Niño summers, widespread rainfall deficits exceeding −200 mm are observed, whereas La Niña 
summers produce surpluses of +200 to +300 mm (Figure 5a). Statistically significant anomalies are widespread across 
northern, western, and southern Thailand, indicati ng a robust ENSO influence during the summer season. This 
pronounced asymmetry underscores ENSO’s critical role in determining pre -monsoon water availability and has 
important implications for agricultural planning and water-resource management. These findings are consistent with 
earlier station-based analyses [6, 38] and more recent regional assessments [5].  
During the rainy season, the fully developed southwest monsoon is strongly modulated by ENSO. In El Niño years, 
rainfall across much of Thailand decre ases substantially, resulting in widespread deficits exceeding −200 mm (Figure 
5b). By contrast, La Niña intensifies rainfall, particularly in the central and northeastern regions and along the eastern 
seaboard, with anomalies exceeding +200 mm. Although d omain-averaged rainfall during La Niña years is slightly 
lower than neutral conditions (Table 2), spatial composites reveal pronounced regional rainfall enhancement, 
particularly across central and northeastern Thailand. This discrepancy indicates that spa tially heterogeneous 
responses may be masked in nationwide averages. Significant anomalies are observed across broad areas of Thailand, 
suggesting that ENSO exerts a widespread influence during the rainy season, affecting large parts of Thailand despite 
regional variability in anomaly magnitude. These findings highlight that ENSO exerts its most pronounced and spatially 
extensive influence during the rainy season. In winter, ENSO’s influence is comparatively weaker and more localized.

5. CONCLUSIONS 
 This research advances previous validation and analytical frameworks by providing a comprehensive evaluation 
of Thailand’s rainfall climatology and ENSO-related variability over the past three decades. The analysis examines the 
reliability of ERA5 precipitation data and quantifies the season-specific impacts of ENSO on regional rainfall patterns. 
These results enhance our understanding of ERA5’s performance as a climate dataset, clarify seasonal and spatial 
rainfall variability in Thailand, and support climate risk management by characterizing ENSO -related hydroclimatic 
fluctuations. Validation against four representative Thai Meteorological Department (TMD) stations indicates that 
ERA5 effectively reproduces both the seasonal cycle and interannual variability of rainfall with moderate to high 
accuracy. Ubon Ratchathani exhibited the highest agreement, with annual R² reaching 0.87, corresponding to regions 
where monsoon circulation is most pronounced. In contrast, the southern peninsula showed lower agreement due to 
complex coastal and orographic influences. Overall, biases remained minor relative  to seasonal totals, reaffirming 
ERA5’s suitability for climatological assessments and ENSO teleconnection analyses in Thailand.  
 The 30 years climatological baseline derived from ERA5 indicates that Thailand’s rainfall is predominantly 
governed by the monsoon season. More than 70% of the annual precipitation occurs during the rainy season, which 
follows a prolonged dry period spanning winter and early summer. Distinct spatial contrasts were clearly represented, 
with enhanced rainfall along the Tenasserim Range and the eastern seaboard resulting from orographic uplift, and 
reduced totals across the central plain and northeastern plateau due to rain -shadow effects. These features 
correspond closely with both ground -based observations and satellite-derived datasets, confirming ERA5’s reliability 
for regional climate analysis. The El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) emerged as one of the dominant modes 
influencing interannual rainfall variability, with El Niño events generally associated with decreased precipit ation, 
particularly during the summer and rainy seasons. Conversely, La Niña events produced enhanced rainfall, particularly 
over the northeastern, central, and southern peninsular regions. Although annual totals exhibited relatively modest 
differences, seasonal anomalies were more pronounced: Table 2 indicates that summer rainfall during La Niña years 
was substantially higher than during El Niño years, reflecting strong seasonal ENSO modulation, while rainy -season 
deficits during El Niño exceeded −200 mm a cross much of the country. Winter effects were comparatively weaker 
overall but remained evident in the southern peninsula, where La Niña enhanced rainfall associated with the 
northeast monsoon. By integrating detailed meteorological data with observational evidence, this study advances the 
understanding of how ENSO influences rainfall dynamics across Thailand. The results reaffirm well -established ENSO 
teleconnections and add value by providing a seasonally explicit, countrywide perspective on rainfall variability across 
Thailand. These contributions strengthen the empirical foundation for applying ENSO related climate information in 
Thailand and across Southeast Asia.  Despite the strengths of the present analysis, several limitations should be 
acknowledged. ERA5 validation was conducted using four representative TMD stations selected to reflect Thailand ’s 
principal climatic regions and provide long-term observational consistency. Although this approach supports regional-
scale assessment, localized rainfall variability associated with urbanization, topographic complexity, and mesoscale 
atmospheric processes may not be fully represented by a single station within each region. Future studies 
incorporating denser observational networks would further improve ERA5  evaluation across Thailand.  In practical 
terms, the study underscores the importance of integrating ENSO patterns into agricultural planning, water -resource 
management, and disaster preparedness strategies. Seasonal forecasts that account for ENSO variabi lity can help 
farmers anticipate dry spells, enable water managers to regulate reservoirs more effectively, and allow policymakers 
to issue timely flood warnings. Thailand can enhance its preparedness for climate change and safeguard food security, 
economic stability, and community wellbeing. These findings are directly applicable to weather-forecasting operations 
and can inform national strategies for climate change adaptation. Moreover, the results provide deeper insight into 
ENSO’s influence on rainfall across Southeast Asia, highlighting the value of regional collaboration in managing climate 
risks and strengthening adaptive capacity.
