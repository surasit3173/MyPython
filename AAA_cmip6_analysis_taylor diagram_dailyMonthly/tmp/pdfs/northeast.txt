ABSTRACT
  The increase in anthropogenic greenhouse gases (GHGs) has resulted in global climate change, 
with the Northeast region of  Thailand experiencing the highest rate of  change. This poses significant 
risks of  drought and its impact on crop yield production. To better understand the potential consequences 
of  climate change and devise suitable adaptation strategies, this study aims to project precipitation 
and temperature data for Northeast Thailand from 2015 to 2055, using bias-corrected global climate 
models under Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs) 4.5 and 8.5. The results showed that 
the annual precipitation and temperature increased from 2015 to 2055 under both RCPs. The annual 
average (minimum-maximum) of  precipitation from the CanESM, GFDL, MIROC5, NorESM, and 
ensemble mean from four models under RCP4.5 were 1,238.83 (904.28-1,629.70), 1,227.82 (865.49-
1,721.31), 1,312.78 (876.60-1,616.38), 1,350.21 (985.55-1,625.00), and 1,282.41 (1,088.43-1,461.49) mm 
and RCP8.5 were 1,267.96 (864.24-1,712.86), 1,222.20 (863.79-1,835.43), 1,294.07 (843.04-1,752.41), 
1,353.14 (1,059.50-1,827.23), and 1,284.34 (1,116.55-1,541.63) mm, respectively. While, those of  
temperature under RCP4.5 were 29.27 (28.15-30.35), 29.59 (27.98-31.20), 29.12 (27.65-30.70), 28.09 
(26.96-29.14), and 29.02 (28.08-29.90) °C and RCP8.5 were 29.50 (28.12-31.40), 29.68 (28.23-31.50), 
29.11 (27.51-30.26), 28.37 (27.11-29.88), and 29.17 (28.08-30.66) °C, respectively. These findings 
suggest that the annual precipitation is lower under RCP4.5 compared to RCP8.5, while the temperature 
shows an increasing trend under both RCPs. Therefore, it is evident that climate change will manifest 
differently in Northeast Thailand, depending on local contexts and the measures implemented today. 
Understanding the impacts and risks of  future climate change at a local scale and identifying adaptive 
solutions pose significant challenges for the future.
Keywords: precipitation, temperature, global climate model, Northeast Thailand

1. INTRODUCTION
Climate change occurs in many areas around 
the world affecting and damaging human life, 
property, natural resources and environment. 
Greenhouse gases from human activities are the 
most significant driver of  observed climate change 
because these gases have the ability to absorb 
infrared energy from the sun. The major results 
from human activities were burning fossil fuels and 
deforestation. Scientific evidence shows that global 
temperature is rising, along with changes occurring 
in precipitation and sea level. During 1960 and 
2000, the average global temperature increased by 
0.6˚C [1]. According to IPCC Sixth Assessment 
Report shown the observed changes in annual 
mean surface temperature in the period 1850–2020 
per °C of  global warming (°C). The maximum 
temperature, average temperature, and minimum 
temperature in the past of  Thailand (1955-2009) 
were increased by 0.57, 0.81, and 0.89˚C, resulting 
in Thailand getting hotter nationwide. The rate 
at which Thailand’s average temperature rose per 
decade (0.174˚C per decade) was higher than the 
global rate (0.126˚C per decade) [2]. Moreover, 
average sea surface temperatures increased at a 
faster rate than in the past, this in turn is related 
to rising sea level. According to data collected by 
water level stations from 1961-2003, the average 
global sea level increased by 1.8 mm a year [3]. 
Moreover, surface temperatures in the Gulf  of  
Thailand and the Andaman Sea rose by 0.1˚C per 
decade from 1976-2006. Sea levels in the Gulf  of  
Thailand also tended to rise. According to data 
collected from 1895-2004 by four sea level stations, 
sea levels in the Gulf  of  Thailand tended to rise 
at a rate of  3.0-5.0 mm per year [4]. Furthermore, 
average annual relative humidity and temperature 
were increasing, while the evaporation rate fell. 
Thailand’s average annual precipitation over the 
past 55 years showed slight decreases, although the 
results were not statistical significance at the 95% 
confidence level. Thus, Chotamonsak, Salath Jr. 
[5] predicted the precipitation in Thailand using 
a WRF reginal climate model. They found that 
heavy precipitation was found mainly along coast 
of  Thailand and Myanmar, with relatively drier 
conditions over the lowlands of  the interior of  
Thailand. In the cool-dry season, heavy precipitation 
was observed only along the southern coast 
and the Malay peninsula. In the hot-dry season, 
the entire region is arid [5]. Thus, the seasonal 
cycle reflects the onset of  south-westerly winds 
associated with the South-Asian monsoon and 
the cyclone season, the primary mechanisms for 
heavy precipitation over the region [5]. Moreover, 
it was found that changes in Thailand’s average 
annual precipitation were related to ENSO, with 
lower average annual precipitation during years 
the El Niño phenomenon occurred, and higher 
amounts of  rain during La Niña [6].
Effects of  climate variability such as 
drought and flood conditions, the weather being 
extremely hot or cold, seasonal shift and change 
have impacted to human and environment. For 
example, increasing global surface temperature is 
the most symbolic parameter of  climate change 
[7] and ambient high temperature, especially 
prolonged extreme high temperature as heatwave 
[8], has caused substantial health burden globally 
[9]. According to study the effect of  heatwave 
was associated with greater cumulative effects on 
cause-specific mortality in Thailand  [10]. These 
regions vulnerable to heatwaves and proportion 
of  elderly population was the major driver behind 
the spatial heterogeneity of  heatwave vulnerability 
[10]. Moreover, previous research in Thailand 
was investigated the effect of  climate change on 
agriculture, for example; rice grown under rainfed 
conditions was found to be highly vulnerable 
to climate change. Moreover, yields of  rice and 
maize are projected to decline as much as 57 and 
44 percent as compared to the normal climate as 
baseline, respectively [11]. Therefore, all sectors 
have attention to preparing to cope and reduce 
the impact that may occur in the near future. 
Especially trend of  average temperature, maximum 
temperature, and minimum temperature in the

and Amnat Charoen) were located in latitude 
14˚7›N to 18˚27’N and longitude 100˚54’E to 
105˚37’E (Figure 1). The total area of  northeast 
in Thailand is 168,854 km

 or 33.17% of  total 
area in Thailand, which is comparable to one-third 
of  the total area of  Thailand. The topography 
is a plateau and slope to the east. There is a hot 
and dry climate, and has moderate precipitation.

2.2 Climate Model
The data in this study consisted of  precipitation 
and temperature data in Northeast, Thailand from 
projections by using the four global climate models 
during 2015 to 2055 under the representative 
concentration pathways (RCPs) and has downscale 
of  the grid cell data to high resolution of  0.1˚ 
or 10 km from the Hydro-Informatics Institute 
(Public Organization) to be suitable for Thailand. 
This indicated that the model’s data in each grid 
has size 0.1x0.1˚ or 100 km
2
/grid (5,094 grid).  
The data accuracy of  the bias correction 
The four global climate models were the 
Canadian Earth System Model (CanESM), which 
is sensitive to the climate equilibrium model due 
to the increased response of  clouds and sea ice. 
The model was able to simulate the pre-industrial 
climate, but it is still important in simulating 
large-scale climate. The second, the Geophysical 
Fluid Dynamics Laboratory’s Model (GFDL) is 
an accurate model enough to study the impacts at 
the local scale. GFDL can observe the evolution 
of  climate in the 20
th
 century and project seasonal. 
The third was the Model for Interdisciplinary 
Research on Climate (MIROC5), which is a 
more-resolution model including more accurate 
patterns of  ocean currents and improvements 
for radiation and turbulence to decrease spatial 
bias in temperature and humidity. This model 
simulates more realistic climate variability than 
previous models. The last one was the Norwegian 
Earth System Model (NorESM) which is a model 
with the turnover of  biogeochemical was applied 
spatially on a medium scale.
RCP is the new greenhouse gas emission 
scenarios, and determined by radiative forcing for 
the world, which economic and social conditions 
and different activities can lead to forcing radiation. 
Therefore, the policy changes in the social, 
technological, and economic situation can be 
compensated by the increase in renewable energy, 
replacing the determined fixed greenhouse gas 
emissions patterns in previous scenarios [14, 15]. 
The selected RCP in this research were RCP4.5 
and RCP8.5. From the Fifth Assessment Report 
(AR5) of  IPCC, RCP8.5 is high emissions consistent 
with the future without policy changes to decrease 
greenhouse gas emissions, high usage of  energy, 
and decrease technology development. RCP 4.5 is 
a mid-range greenhouse gas emission developed 
by the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory 
in the United States. That is consistent with the 
future of  decreased gas emissions but still tends 
to increase [16, 17].
2.3 Map Creation and Data Analysis
Arranged the precipitation and temperature 
data according to the actual latitude and longitude 
coordinates covering Thailand. Precipitation and 
temperature data and the coordinates representative 
of  each square area were 10 x 10 km
2
. Then used 
to create a map that will contour the precipitation 
and temperature data, divided into 0.15 intervals 
by using Surfer 10 (Golden Software, LLC, USA) 
and Quantum GIS version 3.16 (Open Source 
Geospatial Foundation, Chicago, USA) program. 
However, precipitation and temperature data from 
four models under the RCP4.5 and RCP8.5 were 
statistically compared using one -way ANOVA 
in IBM SPSS statistics for Windows, version 
17.0 (IBM Crop., Armonk, N.Y . USA) at a 95% 
significance level. Comparing the climate projection 
and observed weather data was calculated by 
percentage error (% error lower than 5%).

The annual precipitation trend from 2015 to 
2055 found that, annual precipitation increased 
from the past to the future. The annual average 
(minimum-maximum ) of  precipitation from 
the CanESM, GFDL, MIROC5, NorESM, and 
average from four models under RCP4.5 were 
1,238.83(904.28-1,629.70), 1,227.82(865.49-1,721.31), 
1,312.78(876.60-1,616.38), 1,350.21(985.55-1,625.00),

1,222.20(863.79-1,835.43), 1,294.07(843.04-

1,284.34(1,116.55-1,541.63) mm, respectively 
(Figure 2B). However, the annual precipitation 
from CanESM, GFDL, MIROC5, NorESM, and 
average from four models under RCP4.5 were  
lower than RCP8.5.

2015 to 2055 according to precipitation, that were 
increased from the past to the future. The annual

depends on GHG emission scale of  RCP with 
different levels of  carbon dioxide and radiation. 
Thus, the cause of  global warming and climate 
change is the emission of  GHG. Between 1970-
2004, GHG emissions caused by human activity 
increased by 70%, from 28.7 gigatons of  CO 2 
equivalents per year in 1970, to 49 gigatons in 2004, 
with CO2 having the highest rate of  increase from

to 77% of  total emission in 2004 [23]. Hence, the 
GHG mitigation is the best practice for control 
and reduce the global temperature and climate 
change problems in the future.

RCP8.5. The precipitations and temperatures 
under both RCP were no significant different. The 
annual precipitation during 2030 to 2035 under 
RCP4.5 and RCP8.5 increased trend from the 
past approximately 53.08 and 48.19 mm/decade, 
and annual temperature increased trend from

respectively. Thus, the highest and lowest annual 
precipitation provinces under both RCP4.5 and 
RCP8.5 were Bueng Kan with Nakhon Phanom, 
and Nakhon Ratchasima with Chaiyaphum, 
respectively. While, the highest and lowest annual 
temperature provinces under both RCP4.5 and 
RCP8.5 were Nakhon Ratchasima, Surin and 
Nakhon Phanom, respectively.
