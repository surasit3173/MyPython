      Abstract                                               
      Changing climatic conditions have significant consequences for precipitation extremes, es-
      pecially in water-limited regions, such as Cyprus in the Eastern Mediterranean. This study
      evaluates historical and future extreme precipitation using twenty-three models from the
      Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6). The simulations are validated
      against the Climate Hazards Group InfraRed Precipitation with Stations dataset (CHIRPS)
      and combined with bias-corrected projections produced through Quantile Delta Mapping
      (QDM) under the SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios. According to performance evalua-
      tions of models, MRI-ESM2-0 and EC-Earth3-Veg-LR stand out as the top-performing
      models for capturing extreme precipitation behavior over the island. Furthermore, the re-
      sults suggest a long-term decrease in total precipitation and in the number of moderate and
      heavy precipitation days toward the end of the century under both scenarios. At the same
      time, 1-day precipitation maximum generally intensify over most of the island under both
      scenarios, whereas 5-day precipitation maximum increase under SSP2-4.5 but decline un-
      der SSP5-8.5 toward the end of the century. High-percentile indices also strengthen, which
      indicates a future climate where fewer but more powerful events make up a larger share
      of annual precipitation. The duration-based indices also lead to an increase in consecu-
      tive dry days and shorter wet periods toward the end of the century under high emission
      scenario, which together signal a shift toward more prolonged dryness and more severe
      drought events. These combined changes imply that Cyprus will face greater stress on its
      water resources and experience greater flood risks associated with more intense events.
      The results also highlight the importance of region-specific strategies that can improve the
      resilience of water resources under changing climatic conditions.

      1 Introduction                                         
                                                             
      Global warming is altering the hydrological cycle in ways that are already evident worldwide.
      As temperatures rise, the atmosphere holds more moisture and storm systems become less
      stable, which creates conditions that favor heavier precipitation, more prolonged droughts,
      and more frequent heat waves (Trenberth 2011; IPCC et al. 2021a). These extremes ulti-
      mately affect water quality, water availability, ecosystems, and sustainable development
      (Abbott et al. 2019; UN-Water 2020; Zhang et al. 2024a, b). The Intergovernmental Panel
      on Climate Change (IPCC) Sixth Assessment Report reveals that human activities are alter-
      ing climate extremes. Compared with earlier reports, the signals are significantly stronger.
      Since around 1950, most land areas have experienced increases in both the intensity and
      frequency of heavy precipitation events, and these trends continue to track the rise in global
      temperatures (IPCC et al. 2021a).                      
        Projections for the coming decades indicate that the changes in climate are estimated
      to accelerate. Extreme daily precipitation is expected to intensify by about 7% for every
      1 °C of global warming following the Clausius-Clapeyron equation, which governs the
      water-holding capacity of the atmosphere (Trenberth 2011; IPCC et al. 2021a). Mean annual
      precipitation over global land is also projected to increase by 1.5–8% and 1–13% by late
      century under the intermediate and very high greenhouse gas (GHG) emission scenarios
      (i.e., SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5), respectively. If global warming exceeds the ranges assessed
      in these scenarios, changes in climatic impact drivers and extremes are likely to go beyond
      current expectations, which may result in a risk for humans and the ecology, especially
      under SSP5-8.5 (IPCC et al. 2021a). These trends highlight the need for methodologically
      robust approaches to define and examine climate extremes. In this context, climate extremes
      can be characterized through either parametric or non-parametric approaches. The Expert
      Team on Sector-Specific Climate Indices (ET-SCI) defines a wide set of climate indices
      using non-parametric methods. Mainly, the parametric approaches rely on Extreme Value
      Theory to examine the behavior of the most extreme events (Klein Tank et al. 2009; Zhang
      et al. 2011; Kim et al. 2020; IPCC 2021b).             
        The Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP) provides a coordinated multi-model
      framework that helps researchers understand past and future climate behavior (Eyring et al.
      2016). Its latest phase, CMIP6, introduces notable improvements compared with earlier
      generations. The CMIP6 projections use the Scenario Matrix Architecture, which integrates
      the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) that describe alternative socioeconomic devel-
      opments with varying levels of radiative forcing. Two commonly considered pathways in
      the literature are SSP2-4.5, which combines a moderate socioeconomic development with
      a radiative forcing of 4.5 W/m² by 2100, and SSP5-8.5, which combines a fossil-fueled
      development pathway with a radiative forcing of 8.5 W/m² by 2100 (Riahi et al. 2017).
      Many studies show that CMIP6 global climate models represent historical climate features
      more accurately, including Köppen-Geiger climate classes, temperature and precipitation
      patterns, wind fields, and topography-driven precipitation processes. The uncertainties and
      inter-model spread relative to CMIP5 are found to be relatively small (Bağçacı et al. 2021;
      Hamed et al. 2021, 2022; Bayar et al. 2023). Despite these advances, the coarse spatial
      resolution of global models limits their direct use for regional or local analyses. This makes
      downscaling and bias-correction essential for translating large-scale model output to finer
      spatial scales and adjusting systematic errors. Several methods have been used to correct

      precipitation biases, including linear scaling, delta change, distribution mapping, power
      transformation, local intensity scaling, and quantile-based techniques (Teutschbein and
      Seibert 2012; Fang et al. 2015; Daniel 2023). Among these, quantile mapping (QM) and its
      extensions, such as detrended quantile mapping (DQM) and quantile delta mapping (QDM),
      are widely used for daily precipitation as they align the distribution of model outputs with
      observations and preserve projected changes through distributional adjustments (Cannon et
      al. 2015). Comparative studies suggest that QDM and DQM generally perform better than
      traditional QM for reproducing precipitation extremes (Tegegne and Melesse 2020), while
      other studies, such as Gumus et al. (2023), indicate a slight performance advantage of QDM
      over DQM and QM.                                       
        The Mediterranean is recognized as a climate change hotspot (Lionello and Scarascia
      2018). The IPCC AR6 Report projects future decreases in average precipitation, increases in
      heavy precipitation and flooding, and rising aridity and hydrological drought, with medium
      to high confidence (IPCC et al. 2021a). Recent decades show a clear transition toward a
      drier climate in the Eastern Mediterranean and the Middle East, where long-standing pre-
      cipitation variability has begun shifting toward a steady decline. Projections suggest a
      20–30% reduction in precipitation across the Eastern Mediterranean (Zittis et al. 2022). In
      Mediterranean region of Türkiye, decreases of roughly 15% under SSP2-4.5 and 20% under
      SSP5-8.5 are expected (Seker and Gumus 2022), with comparable reductions of up to 20%
      also projected for the Mediterranean and Aegean regions (Gumus et al. 2023). At the same
      time, very heavy precipitation events are projected to become more frequent, even though
      the overall drying trend.                              
        Despite extensive regional evidence of shifting precipitation regimes across the Mediter-
      ranean, only a limited number of climate modeling studies have examined Cyprus in detail.
      Recent analyses of EURO-CORDEX models revealed a consistent drying trend across the
      region. Under the RCP8.5 pathway, Zittis et al. (2021) projected declines in annual pre-
      cipitation and increases in intensity of extreme precipitation events. Lazoglou et al. (2024)
      similarly reported annual precipitation decreases of roughly 20–30% under high emissions,
      along with greater year-to-year variability. Although mean annual precipitation is projected
      to decline, indicators of climate extremes point to increasing variability and intensifica-
      tion of heavy precipitation events. Earlier regional assessments also support this pattern.
      Giannakopoulos et al. (2010), using ENSEMBLES RCM outputs under the A1B scenario,
      found decreasing total precipitation and longer dry spells, especially later in the century.
      Hadjinicolaou et al. (2011) reported increasing consecutive dry days, declining precipita-
      tion frequency in both coastal and inland areas, and a potential rise in precipitation over the
      mountainous regions. Zittis et al. (2020), utilizing WRF simulations, projected declines in
      most extreme precipitation indices and a marked increase in consecutive dry days toward
      the end of the century under the business-as-usual pathway, which contrasts with studies
      suggesting localized increases in extremes. More recent CMIP5-based projections under
      RCP4.5 also suggested decreasing annual precipitation in northern part of island (Elkiran
      et al. 2021). Most recently, Bağçaci et al. (2024), who conducted high-resolution convec-
      tion-permitting WRF simulations using the pseudo-global-warming approach with CMIP6
      GCMs under SSP5-8.5, also found a decrease in seasonal precipitation over Cyprus, with
      the most significant reduction occurring in winter.    
        Studies focused specifically on Cyprus are still limited, especially regarding future
      changes in precipitation extremes. To the authors’ knowledge, no study has examined

      extreme precipitation on the island using CMIP6 global climate models, even though
      CMIP6 provides clear improvements over earlier model generations. This gap is impor-
      tant because Cyprus is highly sensitive to changes in climate, faces increasing pressure on
      its water resources, and lies in a Mediterranean region, where precipitation extremes are
      becoming more common. To address this gap, this study offers a detailed assessment of
      both historical and future precipitation extremes in Cyprus. The main objectives are: (a) to
      evaluate how well CMIP6 models simulate extreme precipitation indices over the island, (b)
      to analyze the temporal and spatial patterns of the multi-model ensemble for the near-future
      (2025–2049), mid-future (2050–2074), and far-future (2075–2099) under the SSP2-4.5 and
      SSP5-8.5 scenarios, and (c) to identify regions of Cyprus that may be more vulnerable
      to more extreme precipitation in the future. By using multiple models and emission sce-
      narios, the study improves our understanding of how precipitation extremes may change on
      a Mediterranean island and provides information that can support climate adaptation and
      risk management at regional scale. While the results help identify broad spatial patterns and
      potentially vulnerable regions across Cyprus, the coarse spatial resolution of GCMs lim-
      its their direct applicability for local-scale planning. Therefore, higher-resolution regional
      climate models or additional statistical or dynamical downscaling are required for detailed
      impact assessments. The model performance evaluation presented in this study can also help
      guide the selection of suitable driving GCMs for future downscaling studies over Cyprus.
      2 Data and methods                                     
                                                             
      2.1 Description of study area                          
                                                             
      Cyprus is located in the Eastern Mediterranean between 32°24′43.59″ and 34°35′20.39″
      longitude and 34°45′15″ and 35°41′49″ latitude, and it covers an area of about 9251 km².
      The island has a typical Mediterranean climate, with hot and dry summers and mild winters
      that receive most of the annual precipitation (Michaelides et al. 2009; Zaifoglu et al. 2019).
      As shown in Fig. 1, Cyprus has a varied landscape that includes the Karpass Peninsula in
      the northeast, the Kyrenia Mountains along the northern coast, the Troodos Mountains in the
      south, and the wide Mesaoria Plain in the center. This diverse topography creates noticeable
      differences in local climate conditions, especially in precipitation patterns across the island.
      For instance, the mean annual precipitation is 500 mm, decreasing to around 300 mm in the
      central areas, while reaching up to 1000 mm in the Troodos mountains. Precipitation occurs
      predominantly between December and February within the wet period, spanning from Octo-
      ber to March (Hadjinicolaou et al. 2011; Zaifoglu et al. 2017; Ghanem and Zaifoglu 2024).
      2.2 Datasets

      imately 5 km) and various temporal resolutions, including daily, pentadal, and monthly,
      from 1981 to the near-present, covering a quasi-global (50° S–50° N, 180° E–180° W)

      2.2.2 CMIP6 global climate models                      
                                                             
      CMIP6 Global Climate Models are employed to analyze both historical and future climate
      conditions. Daily precipitation outputs from 23 CMIP6 GCMs are used for the historical
      period (1981–2010) and for three future time frames: near-term (2025–2049), mid-term
      (2050–2074), and far-term (2075–2099), defined as hydrological years, under two Shared
      Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5). The historical reference period cov-
      ers 30 years, consistent with the WMO standard for climatological normal (WMO 2017).
      The 75-year projection (2025–2099) is divided into three equal 25-year periods to keep
      consistent period lengths throughout the projection. SSP2-4.5 represents an intermediate,
      middle-of-the-road emission trajectory, with radiative forcing reaching 4.5 W/m² by 2100.

      2.3 Methodology                                        
                                                             
      The methodological framework first involves data gathering and pre-processing. Daily pre-
      cipitation outputs from the CMIP6 GCMs are first regridded to the CHIRPS spatial domain
      using nearest-neighbor method and then bias-corrected using the QDM approach. Extreme
      precipitation indices are then calculated for both the raw and bias-corrected datasets, and
      model performance is evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test with CHIRPS
      as the reference. Based on the K–S rankings, model weights are derived for each index and
      used to construct the multi-model ensemble. Ensembles are subsequently generated for the
      historical and future periods. Finally, trends in the ensemble-based extreme precipitation
      indices are assessed using the Modified Mann–Kendall test.

      grid by identifying the center coordinates of each CHIRPS grid cell and extracting the pre-
      cipitation value from the nearest CMIP6 GCM grid cell. This method is chosen to prevent
      additional spatial smoothing of precipitation fields that often results from averaging-based
      interpolation methods, such as bilinear and conservative remapping, particularly at high

      mate indices through a non-parametric approach. These climate indices reflect moderate
      extremes that occur more frequently, in contrast to rarer extremes that have larger recur-
      rence intervals. These indices are widely used in the literature on projecting climate to
      examine the changing climatic conditions and its impact on extremes (Klein Tank et al.

      that both samples originate from an identical distribution. Rejection of the null hypothesis,
      indicating failure of the diagnostic test at a grid, occurs when one or more quantiles of the
      modeled distribution lie outside the 99% confidence interval of the reference quantiles. This

      2.3.4 Formation of multi-model ensemble (MME)          
                                                             
      The rank-based weighting average by Chen et al. (2011) is used to create a MME in this
      study. It incorporates variations in model skills to simulate reference data and is effectively
      used in the projection of climate extremes (Song and Yan 2022; Tang et al. 2021). For each
      extreme index, different model weights are established by taking into account the K–S test
      ranks of all GCMs for that specific index during the reliability factor calculation. The MME
      is then constructed using the derived weights. In this scheme, GCMs that demonstrate stron-
      ger performance for a given index receive higher weights, which makes them more influen-
      tial within the ensemble according to their performance ranking:

      2.3.5 Trend analysis                                   
      The Modified Mann–Kendall test (MMK) (Yue and Wang 2004) is applied to identify sta-
      tistically significant long-term trends in the extreme precipitation indices at the 5% signifi-
      cance level. Trend magnitudes are estimated using Sen’s slope estimator (Sen 1968). The
      classical Mann-Kendall test (Mann 1945; Kendall 1975) is a nonparametric method that
      assumes serial independence in the data (Yue and Wang 2004), which makes the treatment
      of autocorrelation essential prior to its application. In the MMK formulation, the influence
      of serial correlation is addressed through a variance-correction factor, which yields more
      reliable trend estimates (Tošić et al. 2025). The MMK test is widely adopted in hydrological
      and climatic trend analyses (Mallick et al. 2020; Yao et al. 2023; Gomes et al. 2024; Tošić
      et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2025).                          
      3 Results and discussion                               
      3.1 Performance evaluation of CMIP6 models

      3.4 Discussion of results                              
                                                             
      Performance evaluation of CMIP6 GCMs is conducted utilizing the K–S test that evaluates
      the agreement between the distribution of raw GCM precipitation and CHIRPS observa-
      tions at each grid point. After nearest-neighbor regridding, each GCM cell value is assigned
      to all CHIRPS grid points within that cell without interpolation, so that coarser models
      produce a larger number of grid points sharing identical daily values. In principle, this may
      lead to models passing the K–S test more easily. However, the K–S test passing rates do
      not indicate a systematic advantage in resolution. GCMs show considerable variation in
      performance across different extreme precipitation indices. While a specific model shows
      good performance with high passing rates for one index, it can exhibit poor performance
      for another index, even when the spatial resolution remains constant. This variability indi-
      cates that the physics of the model and its ability to reflect regional precipitation processes
      are more important factors for the model performance in the K–S test than the sole native
      resolution of the model.                               
        These patterns are examined for representative indices from each index category by
      relating model performance to model resolution and the seasonal characteristics of precipi-
      tation in Cyprus. Percentile-based indices, such as R95p, demonstrate high passing rates
      across all models and resolutions because their thresholds are not fixed absolute values
      but are obtained from precipitation data of each model. For indices that depend on fixed
      thresholds or event characteristics, performance varies significantly within each resolution
      group, classified as coarse (≥ 2°), medium (1.26–1.88°), and fine (≤ 1.25°) resolution. For
      R10mm, the fine-resolution model performs best, but coarse-resolution models also rank
      among the top performers, while other fine-resolution models achieve significantly lower
      passing rates. Since these events mainly occur during the wet season between October and
      March (Michaelides et al. 2009), the model performance is influenced more by its ability
      to accurately represent the distribution of wet-season intensity than by its grid spacing.
      For Rx1day, the relatively higher passing rates are obtained by medium-resolution models
      rather than by the finest-resolution ones. This situation indicates that capturing the maxi-
      mum daily precipitation during the wet season is more influenced by the characteristics of
      the model than by its resolution. Although the remarkable variability across models, Rx5day
      shows significantly higher passing rates than Rx1day, since multi-day accumulation reduces
      sensitivity to individual peak days. For CDD, both fine- and coarse-resolution models rank
      among the best performers, reflecting the impact of the prolonged dry summer character-
      istic of the Mediterranean, with both the best- and worst-performing models covering the
      1 3                                                    

===== PAGE 23 =====
                                                             
                                                             
                                                             
      Natural Hazards (2026) 122:368         Page 23 of 33 368
                                                             
      full resolution range. In contrast, CWD consistently performs poorly across all models and
      resolutions. While CDD measures the duration of dry periods dominated by the prolonged
      dry summer, CWD shows the continuity of wet periods during the rainy season. This con-
      trast implies that models are more effective at capturing large-scale, persistent seasonal
      trends rather than the day-to-day variations in wet-season precipitation. Models with similar
      resolutions can vary significantly in passing rates for all categories, indicating that model
      physics and the representation of regional precipitation processes are more important than
      native resolution alone. This index-dependent variability is considered by creating MME
      projections through an index-specific rank-based weighting approach (Chen et al. 2011).
      All 23 GCMs contribute to the MME of each index, and no model is excluded. The overall
      ranking of the five highest-performing models reported earlier serves as a general summary
      and is not used as a model selection criterion in the ensemble construction.
        The strong seasonal changes in Cyprus’s precipitation should be also considered while
      interpreting the performance of the models and the changes in future projections. Similar to
      other Mediterranean climates, most precipitation occurs during the wet season and the most
      intense events are usually related to winter Mediterranean cyclones. Therefore, although
      the ET-SCI extreme precipitation indices used in this study were calculated on an annual
      basis, they are largely controlled by precipitation events during the winter months. Indices,
      such as Rx1day, Rx5day, R95p, and R99p, are mainly influenced by intense winter storms,
      while frequency-based indices (e.g., R10mm and R20mm) also mostly reflect precipitation
      events during the wet season. This strong seasonal pattern also affects the model evaluation
      results, since the statistical performance of the GCMs mainly reflects their ability to simu-
      late winter precipitation variability and extremes. For this reason, the projected changes in
      extreme precipitation indices should primarily be interpreted as potential changes in the
      intensity and frequency of winter precipitation events over Cyprus rather than as uniform
      changes throughout the year. Furthermore, percentile-based indices represent relative posi-
      tions within the precipitation distribution. Therefore, they are less sensitive to biases in the
      absolute magnitude of precipitation. In contrast, indices such as Rx1day, CWD, and SDII
      depend more directly on the individual storm events and wet spell persistence. Since pre-
      cipitation in Cyprus is strongly concentrated in the winter wet season, the performance of
      these indices mainly reflects how well the models reproduce winter precipitation variability
      and associated storm processes.                        
        In this study, the nearest-neighbor method is chosen for regridding specifically to avoid
      additional spatial smoothing during the spatial mapping (Rajulapati et al. 2021). Then, QDM
      bias correction adjusts the statistical distribution at each grid point against the reference
      CHIRPS dataset (Cannon et al. 2015). Although this procedure effectively maps GCM pre-
      cipitation data onto the high-resolution CHIRPS reference grid and corrects distributional
      biases, it does not create variability at sub-grid scales or represent the physical feedbacks
      that influence the climate response at resolutions finer than the driving GCM (Maraun 2016).
      The trend-preserving nature of the QDM approach causes projected changes in extreme pre-
      cipitation at the local scale to be entirely taken from the driving GCMs. This means that bias
      correction cannot handle inherent deficiencies in the GCMs themselves (Maraun and Wid-
      mann 2018; Casanueva et al. 2020). Additionally, statistical bias correction assumes that the
      statistical relationships identified under current climate conditions will also hold in future
      climates (Gutierrez et al. 2022). Although QDM considers this to some extent by preserving
      trends, it is still a significant challenge for all statistical downscaling methods.

      368 Page 24 of 33                  Natural Hazards (2026) 122:368
                                                             
        The use of CHIRPS as the only observational reference dataset creates an additional
      source of uncertainty. CHIRPS has been validated against ground-based observations in
      Cyprus and demonstrates strong agreement with station data for percentile-based thresh-
      olds, frequency indices, SDII, and average precipitation values (Katsanos et al. 2016a, b).
      However, the study also showed that duration indices performed poorly. Specifically, CDD
      showed a poor correlation with station data, whereas CWD showed a moderate correlation.
      As a combined satellite-gauge product, CHIRPS can underestimate precipitation variance in
      some regions because the final dataset partially preserves the low variance of the source sat-
      ellite estimates (Funk et al. 2015). While any systematic bias in the reference dataset affects
      the model performance evaluation results because the K–S test compares GCM distributions
      to CHIRPS, observational biases are incorporated into the bias-corrected outputs in QDM
      due to the adjustment of GCM distributions to match CHIRPS distributions. However, since
      QDM preserves the relative changes projected by the GCMs, the direction and magnitude
      of projected trends are less sensitive to absolute biases in the reference data. Future research
      could be improved by comparing findings across several independent observational datasets
      to more precisely evaluate this source of uncertainty. 
        Accordingly, the bias-corrected projections provided in this study might be considered as
      reasonable estimates of changes in regional precipitation, based on the large-scale climate
      signals generated by the GCMs, rather than as precise local forecasts (Maraun and Wid-
      mann 2018). Statistical and dynamical downscaling can be considered as complementary
      approaches. While statistical approaches are computationally less demanding and provide
      the ability to work with multiple GCM datasets and scenarios, dynamical downscaling can
      reflect local climate feedback mechanisms that statistical methods tend to simplify (Reder
      et al. 2025). The GCMs that show good performance across extreme precipitation indices
      in this study can be utilized as suitable driving models for future high-resolution regional
      climate simulations over Cyprus.                       
        For the extreme precipitation intensity, Gumus et al. (2023), using CMIP6 GCMs, report
      that indices are expected to increase across the Mediterranean region of Türkiye by the end
      of the century under both scenarios, and the increases become stronger under SSP5-8.5.
      Lazoglou et al. (2024), based on EURO-CORDEX simulations, describe a more moder-
      ate response for Cyprus, where most intensity indices exhibit little change and only slight
      increases appear under the high radiative forcing scenario Although both studies project
      increasing extreme precipitation intensity under the high-emission scenario by the end of
      the century, the magnitude of the projected increase is larger in the present study. In con-
      trast, Zittis et al. (2020), using WRF regional climate model simulations, show that Rx5day
      is projected to decline toward the end of the century under a high-emission pathway. Their
      strongest decrease occurs at the peak of the Troodos Mountains, which aligns with the find-
      ings of the present study. While intensity indices decrease in high-elevation areas, they
      generally increase in the north-eastern part of Cyprus, except for Rx5day, toward the end of
      the century. Such increases imply that precipitation events may become more intense even if
      total precipitation declines, which raises the likelihood of more frequent and severe flooding
      events and amplifies associated economic and environmental risks. The behavior of Rx1day
      and Rx5day can be explained by the thermodynamic and dynamic contributions to extreme
      precipitation identified by Pfahl et al. (2017). As temperatures rise, the increasing atmo-
      spheric moisture capacity leads to more intense individual storms (Trenberth 2011; Pfahl
      et al. 2017), which is consistent with the projected increase in Rx1day in most of Cyprus.
      1 3

      However, the projected decline in Mediterranean cyclone frequency (Flaounas et al. 2022)
      may reduce multi-day precipitation accumulations, leading to widespread Rx5day decline
      under the high-emission scenario toward the end of the century. In the Troodos Mountains,
      where topography produces a steep precipitation gradient from approximately 300 mm on
      the lowland plains to up to 1000 mm in the mountains (Hadjinicolaou et al. 2011; Ghanem
      and Zaifoglu 2024), the projected weakening of Cyprus Lows (Hochman et al. 2018; Zittis
      et al. 2022) may reduce moisture transport to high elevations sufficiently that even 1-day
      precipitation maximum decline toward the end of the century alongside 5-day precipita-
      tion. This shows that dynamic influence can locally suppress thermodynamic condensa-
      tion in mountainous regions. Overall, the findings imply that thermodynamic intensification
      dominates over lower-elevation areas of Cyprus. However, as the forcing increases, over
      longer timelines, and at higher elevations, the role of dynamic weakening becomes more
      pronounced, consistent with the thermodynamic-dynamic framework (Pfahl et al. 2017).
      Moreover, regarding the trends, previous studies, that used station-based observational data
      with variable periods, report mixed findings for intensity indices in Cyprus. Kostopoulou
      and Jones (2005) detected mostly insignificant trends in intensity indices, while others
      identify increasing (Zaifoğlu et al. 2017) or decreasing tendencies (Mathbout et al. 2018).
      Besides, Lazoglou et al. (2024), which used gridded observational data for the trend analy-
      sis, show weak or insignificant historical trends. Future projections also show considerable
      variability. Zittis et al. (2021) projected insignificant trends for Rx1day in the 21st century
      in Cyprus under the high emission scenario, although a decreasing tendency was identified
      for Nicosia. Hadjinicolaou et al. (2011) found insignificant trends for intensity indices in the
      near-future period under the medium emission scenario, while these trends are increasing
      for Limassol and Saittas, and decreasing for Nicosia. These differences from the present
      study may arise from variations in the datasets used, the study area, and the simulation
      period.                                                
        Previous studies indicate that precipitation frequency indices tend to decline toward the
      end of the century. Similar decreases have been reported for the Mediterranean region of
      Türkiye under both medium and high emission scenarios (Gumus et al. 2023), as well as for
      Cyprus in the near-future period under a medium forcing level and in the far-future period
      under a high forcing level (Hadjinicolaou et al. 2011; Lazoglou et al. 2024), which is consis-
      tent with the findings of the present study. Zittis et al. (2020) also states that R20mm is pro-
      jected to decrease by the end of the century under high radiative forcing, and identifies the
      strongest reductions near the peak of the Troodos Mountains. This pattern aligns with the
      results of this study, which also indicates that the Troodos region experiences the most pro-
      nounced decline in R20mm. Furthermore, previous studies show considerable variability in
      frequency trends across Cyprus and the wider region. Some studies reported weak or insig-
      nificant historical trends (Zaifoğlu et al. 2017; Zittis 2017; Lazoglou et al. 2024), which are
      consistent with the results of the present study, while others identified significant decreases
      (Kostopoulou and Jones 2005; Mathbout et al. 2018). Hadjinicolaou et al. (2011) also found
      insignificant decreasing trends in the near-future period under a medium-emission scenario,
      although their analysis was limited to a small number of stations. The projected decrease in
      R10mm and R20mm is physically consistent with the poleward expansion of the subtropi-
      cal dry zone and the associated decline in Mediterranean cyclone frequency under warming
      conditions (IPCC 2021c; Flaounas et al. 2022), which reduce the number of days exceeding
      moderate and heavy precipitation thresholds. This reduction has important implications for

      water resources, as steady moderate precipitation allows time for infiltration into the soil,
      whereas high-intensity extreme events predominantly generate surface runoff (Trenberth et
      al. 2003).                                             
        The results regarding duration indices align with previous studies conducted in Cyprus,
      Türkiye, Greece, and the broader Eastern Mediterranean, despite differences in study peri-
      ods, models, and scenarios. These studies consistently project a more pronounced increase
      in CDD by the end of the century under medium and high emission pathways, as well as
      slight decreases or stable patterns for CWD (Giannakopoulos et al. 2010; Hochman et al.
      2017, 2018; Zittis et al. 2020; Georgoulias et al. 2022; Gumus et al. 2023; Lazoglou et al.
      2024). Longer dry periods combined with shorter wet periods imply a shift toward greater
      aridity across the region. In terms of trends, several studies identified significant increases
      in historical dry and wet spell durations (Kostopoulou and Jones 2005; Mathbout et al.
      2018), while others reported weak or insignificant trends. For example, Zittis (2017) found
      an insignificant upward trend in CDD and no trend in CWD for 1988–2014, which is con-
      sistent to the present results, and Lazoglou et al. (2024) reported an insignificant decrease in
      CDD and no trend in CWD for 1981–2018. Hadjinicolaou et al. (2011) found a significant
      increase in CDD only in Limassol for the near future under a medium-emission scenario.
      The poleward expansion of the subtropical dry zone under greenhouse gas forcing (IPCC
      2021c) provides a large-scale mechanism for the changes in duration indices. Cyprus is
      located where subtropical and midlatitude atmospheric circulation regimes converge, which
      makes it particularly exposed to shifts in regional precipitation patterns (IPCC 2022). As the
      dry zone expands, the dry season may become longer, while the decreasing frequency of
      Mediterranean cyclones (IPCC 2021c; Flaounas et al. 2022) may result in shorter wet peri-
      ods during the rainy season. In the near future period, internal climate variability can mask
      or oppose the drying signal over decadal timescales (Hawkins and Sutton 2011).
        Gumus et al. (2023) report that percentile and contribution indices are expected to
      increase by the end of the century across the Mediterranean region of Türkiye for both emis-
      sion scenarios. Their results indicate that increases are stronger for R95pTOT and R99pTOT
      under SSP5-8.5, while R95p and R99p do not differ substantially between scenarios. In
      contrast, the present study shows that R95p decreases under SSP2-4.5, and increases under
      SSP5-8.5 toward the end of the century. The remaining percentile indices display a clearer
      increase by the end of the century, particularly under SSP5-8.5, which is consistent with the
      stronger forcing associated with the high-emissions pathway. Additionally, some studies
      identified upward trends, although statistical significance was limited to a small number of
      stations (Zaifoğlu et al. 2017; Katsanos et al. 2017). Other studies reported mostly insignifi-
      cant historical trends (Kostopoulou and Jones 2005) or predominantly significant decreases,
      as in the analysis of Mathbout et al. (2018) for 1962–2012. This study shows partial consis-
      tency with earlier results in terms of positive trends, however, most indices show significant
      trends. The increases in most high-percentile and contribution indices reflect the thermo-
      dynamic intensification of extreme precipitation under warming (Trenberth 2011; Pfahl et
      al. 2017), with a stronger response under SSP5-8.5 consistent with the higher radiative
      forcing. The increase in R95pTOT and R99pTOT indicates that an increasing proportion of
      annual precipitation is concentrated in the most intense events (Trenberth et al. 2003), which
      implies greater hydrological variability even as total precipitation declines.
        For the total precipitation, studies conducted across Mediterranean Basin countries con-
      sistently show that total precipitation is projected to decline by the end of the century under

      medium and high emission scenarios, with a more pronounced decrease under high emis-
      sions. These findings support the results obtained in this study. Several studies also report
      reductions in total precipitation in the early-century period for both scenarios (Giannako-
      poulos et al. 2010; Onol and Unal 2014; Georgoulias et al. 2022; Zittis et al. 2022; Seker
      and Gumus 2022; Gumus et al. 2023; Lazoglou et al. 2024). In Mediterranean region of
      Türkiye, decreases in total precipitation are projected under both CMIP6 emission scenarios
      by the end of the century, with more pronounced decreases under SSP5-8.5 (Seker and
      Gumus 2022; Gumus et al. 2023). Onol and Unal (2014) also projected a decrease in the
      Mediterranean region of Türkiye under high emission scenario by the end of the century.
      Zittis et al. (2022) exhibit that total precipitation is projected to decrease by the end of the
      century under CMIP5 high emission scenario in the Eastern Mediterranean and Middle
      East region, while the decrease is more pronounced in the regions near the Mediterranean
      Sea. In Cyprus, Giannakopoulos et al. (2010), who used ENSEMBLES simulations, show
      that total precipitation is expected to decrease in the near- and far-future periods under
      the medium emission scenario, while Lazoglou et al. (2024) found a decrease by the end
      of the century under the high emission scenario. Another EURO-CORDEX research also
      projected a decrease in total precipitation by the end of the century under both medium and
      high emission scenarios in the Mediterranean islands, with a more distinct decrease under
      the high emission scenario (Georgoulias et al. 2022). A decrease in total annual precipitation
      can raise drought risk in the region because less precipitation means less runoff and slower
      recovery of groundwater and reservoirs. It also makes it harder for agricultural and natural
      ecosystems to cope with long dry periods. These water shortages can build up over time
      and lead to longer and more severe dry spells, especially in semi-arid areas where higher
      temperatures increase evapotranspiration. The decline in total precipitation over Cyprus
      is consistent with the large-scale drying trend projected for the Mediterranean Basin. The
      poleward expansion of the subtropical dry zone and weakening of winter storm patterns
      under radiative forcing affect this trend (IPCC 2021c). The increase in near-future under
      SSP2-4.5 can result from internal climate variability, which can dominate the forced signal
      over decadal periods, but it does not change the long-term drying trend (Hawkins and Sut-
      ton 2011). The stronger decline under SSP5-8.5 is consistent with the projected reduction
      in cyclone frequency under high-emission conditions (Hochman et al. 2018). Moreover,
      the temporal distribution of precipitation is also as crucial as the total amount for surface
      hydrology. When total precipitation declines but individual events become more intense, a
      larger fraction of rainfall is lost to surface runoff rather than infiltrating the soil (Trenberth
      et al. 2003), which further reduces the effective water supply. Zittis et al. (2021) projected
      significant future decreases in the 21st century under the high-emission scenario, while the
      present study found insignificant decreases in all future periods under the SSP5-8.5. Near-
      future trends under medium-emission scenarios have been reported as insignificant or weak
      in earlier study, which aligns with findings from Hadjinicolaou et al. (2011), however, a
      significantly increasing trend is projected in the near-future period under SSP2-4.5 in the
      present study. Historical trends have also been predominantly insignificant, with positive
      tendencies reported for Northern Cyprus and neighboring Mediterranean islands (Zaifoğlu
      et al. 2017; Stathi et al. 2023), which is consistent with the present results.

      3.5 Limitations and future work                        
                                                             
      The projections presented in this study use statistical downscaling through nearest-neighbor
      regridding and QDM bias correction of coarse GCM outputs. However, these methods do
      not account for sub-grid-scale variability or depict local climate feedbacks that dynamical
      downscaling can simulate (Maraun 2016; Reder et al. 2025). Because QDM maintains the
      climate change signal from the driving GCMs, projected changes at the local scale depend
      on the trends generated by these models, and bias correction cannot correct fundamental
      deficiencies in the models (Maraun and Widmann 2018; Casanueva et al. 2020). The use of
      CHIRPS as the sole observational reference also creates uncertainty, particularly for dura-
      tion indices where CHIRPS demonstrates weaker agreement with station data (Katsanos et
      al. 2016a, b). Later investigations may utilize the top-performing GCMs from the present
      study as input models for high-resolution dynamical downscaling over Cyprus and integrate
      multiple observational datasets to minimize uncertainty.
                                                             
      4 Conclusion                                           
                                                             
      This study assessed CMIP6 model performance and projected changes in extreme precipita-
      tion over Cyprus under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 after QDM correction. The results reveal
      a transition toward a drier climatic condition that features more pronounced short-duration
      extremes, longer dry periods, and increasing hydrological instability. The main conclusions
      are summarized below:                                  
                                                             
      ●  MRI-ESM2-0 and EC-Earth3-Veg-LR are the top-performing CMIP6 models for rep-
         resenting extreme precipitation indices over Cyprus, which provides a stronger foun-
         dation for extreme precipitation evaluation and downstream hydrological applications.
      ●  Total precipitation decreases in both emission scenarios, which means that water avail-
         ability becomes more limited over time. This decrease reduces seasonal water stor-
         age, slows groundwater recharge, and makes the island more vulnerable to precipitation
         shortages during the wet season. These changes are especially important for Cyprus,
         where natural freshwater resources are already scarce, evapotranspiration is high, and
         water systems are highly sensitive to year-to-year variability.
      ●  The intensity of extreme precipitation increases over most of the Cyprus, especially
         under SSP5-8.5, which shows that short lasting intense events become more severe.
         Higher Rx1day values lead to higher peak flows, faster runoff, and a greater risk of flash
         floods, especially in steep or urban areas. This increase in single-day intensity may also
         put pressure on infrastructure designed for past precipitation conditions. While Rx5day
         increases under SSP2-4.5, it declines toward the end of the century under SSP5-8.5,
         particularly in the Troodos Mountains.              
      ●  The projected decrease in the number of heavy and very heavy precipitation days in-
         dicates that such events become less common in future climate scenarios. At the same
         time, the increase in precipitation intensity suggests that precipitation may occur through
         fewer but stronger events. When very intense precipitation occurs, a large portion of the
         water is converted to surface runoff, while only a limited amount infiltrates into the soil
         and contributes to groundwater recharge. In addition, the projected decrease in total
      1 3                                                    

===== PAGE 29 =====
                                                             
                                                             
                                                             
      Natural Hazards (2026) 122:368         Page 29 of 33 368
                                                             
         precipitation accumulated during wet days indicates a reduction in the overall water
         input to the land surface, which can reduce soil moisture and limit infiltration processes
         that support aquifer recharge.                      
      ●  High percentile indices generally increase under SSP5-8.5, which shows that very heavy
         events contribute a larger share of annual totals. Higher R99p and R99pTOT values re-
         veal that annual totals become more concentrated in a small number of intense events.
         This shift increases variability and leads to faster runoff responses.
      ●  Duration indices exhibit that dry spells become longer and wet periods become shorter,
         particularly under the high emission scenario. CDD increases notably by the end of the
         century under SSP5-8.5, while changes remain marginal, with even declining trends in
         the near- and mid- future periods under SSP2-4.5. Even though CWD rises slightly late
         in the century under SSP2-4.5, the overall trend still implies more persistent dryness
         and a higher risk of severe droughts, especially under high forcing, which may intensify
         water stress across Cyprus.                         
      These conclusions emphasize that the changes in both total precipitation and extreme events
      suggest that Cyprus is moving toward a climate with less overall precipitation but more
      concentrated and impactful extremes. This emerging pattern highlights the need for region-
      specific adaptation, such as better drought planning in the lowlands and more effective flood
      protection in the northern and mountainous areas.
