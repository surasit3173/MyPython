[7] Contrasting Seasonal Rainfall Trends under Predominantly Stable Annual Conditions: Evidence from Trend-Free Pre-Whitening Analysis in Prachuap Khiri Khan Province, Thailand
[8] 
[9]  Abstract
[10] Statistically stable annual rainfall can mask substantial shifts in seasonal rainfall distribution, obscuring hydroclimatically important changes in rainfall timing and seasonal water availability. This study investigated annual, wet-season, and dry-season rainfall trends at 12 rain-gauge stations in Prachuap Khiri Khan Province, coastal western Thailand, using daily rainfall records from 1981–2014. Serial autocorrelation was evaluated prior to trend detection, and rainfall trends were analysed using a conditional Trend-Free Pre-Whitening Mann–Kendall (TFPW-MK) framework with Sen's slope and rank-based 95% confidence intervals. Among 36 station–timescale combinations, seven (19.4%) exhibited statistically significant trends. Annual rainfall remained predominantly stable, with a significant increase detected only at station 500002 (+9.18 mm yr⁻¹). In contrast, significant wet-season declines occurred at three stations (−5.61 to −7.62 mm yr⁻¹), whereas three stations showed significant dry-season increases (+5.22 to +6.78 mm yr⁻¹). The strongest seasonal contrast occurred at station 500006, where significant wet-season drying and dry-season wetting coexisted despite the absence of a significant annual trend, providing evidence consistent with intra-annual rainfall redistribution. Accounting for serial dependence also altered trend inference for wet-season rainfall at station 500003, where TFPW-MK identified a significant decline that was not detected by the original Mann–Kendall test. These results demonstrate that seasonal rainfall redistribution, rather than changes in annual rainfall totals, represents the dominant expression of recent rainfall variability in the study area. Annual rainfall alone therefore provides an incomplete basis for assessing hydroclimatic change, underscoring the importance of season-specific rainfall analysis for water-resource management and climate adaptation in monsoon-influenced coastal regions.

[14] 1. Introduction
[15] Reliable seasonal rainfall underpins water security, agricultural production, ecosystem functioning, and climate adaptation in monsoon regions. However, rainfall change is not always expressed as a simple increase or decrease in annual rainfall totals (Trenberth et al, 2003). A location may exhibit statistically stable annual rainfall while undergoing substantial changes in the seasonal distribution, timing, or persistence of precipitation (Palizdan et al, 2015). Such changes can alter reservoir recharge, soil-moisture replenishment, irrigation scheduling, and seasonal water availability despite little apparent change in annual rainfall (Ahmad et al, 2015). Consequently, analyses based solely on annual rainfall totals may overlook hydroclimatically important changes that are directly relevant to environmental management and water-resource planning (IPCC, 2023).
[16] Thailand's rainfall is governed by the interaction of the Southwest and Northeast monsoons, tropical weather disturbances, regional moisture transport, and complex topography, resulting in pronounced spatial and seasonal variability, particularly in coastal regions (Arai et al, 2019). Previous studies have reported substantial geographical and seasonal heterogeneity in rainfall across Thailand and Southeast Asia (Limsakul et al., 2017; Fiaz et al., 2025), including changes in rainfall concentration, seasonal timing, rainy-season duration, and contrasting annual and seasonal trends. These findings suggest that annual rainfall totals alone may not adequately capture ongoing hydroclimatic change and emphasize the importance of examining seasonal rainfall behaviour to improve understanding of environmental change in monsoon-influenced regions.
[17] Prachuap Khiri Khan Province is well suited for investigating seasonal rainfall variability because its coastal setting and diverse physiography create pronounced spatial differences in rainfall over relatively short distances (Harmoko et al., 2026). Such heterogeneity provides an opportunity to examine how seasonal rainfall behaviour varies among stations within a relatively compact monsoon-influenced region. Improved understanding of these variations is important for supporting water-resource management, agriculture, and climate adaptation in coastal western Thailand.
[18] Although rainfall variability in Thailand has been widely investigated at national and regional scales, relatively few studies have examined whether statistically stable annual rainfall conceals contrasting seasonal changes at individual monitoring stations in coastal western Thailand. Most previous studies have focused on annual rainfall trends or regional averages, which may mask localized seasonal responses (Deng et al., 2025). Consequently, it remains unclear whether apparent annual stability reflects genuine hydroclimatic stability or results from opposing wet- and dry-season trends that offset one another. Addressing this knowledge gap is important because changes in seasonal rainfall distribution may substantially affect water availability and environmental management without producing detectable changes in annual rainfall totals.
[19] Another important consideration is the influence of serial autocorrelation on trend detection (Burn and Elnur, 2002). The Mann–Kendall (MK) test is widely used for detecting monotonic trends in hydroclimatic time series because it is robust and requires no assumption of normality (Mann, 1945; Kendall, 1975). However, the test assumes serially independent observations, and positive autocorrelation may affect statistical inference by altering the variance of the test statistic (Hamed and Rao, 1998). Trend-Free Pre-Whitening (TFPW) has been widely adopted to reduce the influence of lag-1 serial dependence while preserving the underlying monotonic trend (Yue et al., 2002). Because serial autocorrelation is not present in every rainfall series, applying pre-whitening indiscriminately may introduce unnecessary data transformation, whereas ignoring significant persistence may reduce the reliability of trend detection. Therefore, this study adopted a conditional TFPW framework in which serial autocorrelation was evaluated before trend analysis, and pre-whitening was applied only to rainfall series exhibiting significant lag-1 autocorrelation (Do, 2026).
[20] Against this background, this study examined annual, wet-season, and dry-season rainfall trends using daily rainfall records from 12 rain-gauge stations in Prachuap Khiri Khan Province during 1981–2014. The objectives were to (1) determine whether annual rainfall exhibited significant monotonic trends, (2) examine whether seasonal analyses revealed contrasting wet- and dry-season trends that were not evident from annual rainfall totals, and (3) assess whether accounting for significant serial autocorrelation influenced trend detection relative to the original Mann–Kendall (MK) test. By integrating seasonal rainfall analysis with a conditional Trend-Free Pre-Whitening Mann–Kendall (TFPW-MK) framework, Sen's slope estimation, and station-scale spatial analysis, this study investigated whether apparently stable annual rainfall concealed environmentally important seasonal changes. The findings provide a more comprehensive understanding of rainfall variability in coastal western Thailand and offer information to support water-resource management, agricultural planning, and climate-change adaptation in monsoon-influenced environments.
[21] 2. Study Area and Methods
[22] 2.1 Study area
[23] Prachuap Khiri Khan Province is located along the western coast of the Gulf of Thailand in upper southern Thailand and covers approximately 6,368 km². The province extends in a narrow north–south direction, bordered by the Gulf of Thailand to the east and the Tenasserim Range along the Myanmar border to the west. This distinctive physiographic setting creates pronounced topographic gradients over relatively short distances. These gradients strongly influence local rainfall through interactions among the southwest monsoon, coastal moisture transport, and orographic uplift.
[24] The study area encompasses four major physiographic settings: coastal lowlands bordering the Gulf of Thailand, inland alluvial plains, upland and foothill areas associated with the Tenasserim mountain system, and offshore islands in the Gulf of Thailand. Together, these contrasting environments produce substantial variations in elevation, distance from the coastline, and exposure to prevailing monsoon airflow, allowing nearby stations to experience markedly different rainfall regimes.
[25] 2.2 Rainfall data and temporal aggregation
[26] Daily rainfall observations from twelve rain-gauge stations located across Prachuap Khiri Khan Province were obtained for the period from 1 January 1981 to 31 December 2014 (Table 1; Figure 1). The station network provides representative coverage of the province's four principal physiographic environments—coastal lowlands, inland alluvial plains, upland areas, and offshore islands—allowing rainfall variability to be evaluated across contrasting geographical settings.
[27] Before analysis, the rainfall records were screened for completeness and temporal continuity to ensure consistent time-series construction. Daily rainfall was then aggregated into three temporal scales: annual (January–December), wet season (May–October), and dry season (November–April). The annual and wet-season datasets each comprised 34 observations spanning 1981–2014, exceeding the 30-year minimum recommended by the World Meteorological Organization for climate normals and long-term climate analyses (WMO, 2017). Because the dry season extends across two calendar years, rainfall totals were accumulated from November of one year through April of the next. As observations began on 1 January 1981, the first complete dry season could not be constructed, yielding a dataset of 33 complete November–April seasons.

[67] 2.6 Spatial representation of rainfall trends
[68] Spatial patterns of rainfall trends were mapped using station-based Sen's slope estimates derived from annual and seasonal rainfall series. Continuous trend surfaces were generated by inverse distance weighting (IDW; power = 2), clipped to the administrative boundary of Prachuap Khiri Khan Province, and overlaid with station locations indicating trend direction and statistical significance (α = 0.05).
[69] IDW is a deterministic interpolation method and was used only to visualize broad spatial patterns in rainfall trends. Because the interpolated surfaces are influenced by station density and distribution, particularly within the province's sparse monitoring network and heterogeneous coastal terrain, they should not be interpreted as statistically reliable estimates for unsampled locations. Statistical significance was assessed exclusively from the station-level TFPW–MK results, whereas the interpolated maps served only as visual support for regional pattern interpretation. All analyses, including serial-correlation screening, conditional TFPW–MK, Sen's slope estimation with rank-based confidence intervals, and IDW mapping, were performed in Python 3.12 using NumPy, SciPy, and pandas for statistical analyses, and Matplotlib and GeoPandas for spatial visualization.

[71] 3. Results and Discussion
[72] 3.1 Rainfall variability and serial dependence
[73] Substantial spatial differences in rainfall characteristics were observed across the 12 monitoring stations, reflecting the heterogeneous hydroclimatic setting of Prachuap Khiri Khan Province (Table 1). Mean annual rainfall ranged from 944.1 mm yr⁻¹ at station 500202 to 1,376.2 mm yr⁻¹ at station 500002, whereas the coefficient of variation (CV) ranged from 9.6% at station 500008 to 27.1% at station 500001. Stations with similar mean annual rainfall did not necessarily exhibit comparable interannual variability. For example, stations 500008 and 500201 received similar annual rainfall totals (1,206.3 and 1,108.2 mm yr⁻¹, respectively), but their variability differed markedly (CV = 9.6% and 24.5%, respectively). These results indicate considerable spatial heterogeneity in rainfall variability across the province.
[74] Serial dependence also varied among stations and temporal scales. Of the 36 station–timescale series analysed, seven (19.4%) exhibited significant lag-1 autocorrelation and therefore required conditional Trend-Free Pre-Whitening (TFPW) prior to trend analysis. All autocorrelated series occurred in the annual or wet-season datasets, whereas none of the dry-season series showed significant lag-1 autocorrelation. This pattern indicates that rainfall persistence differed among seasonal timescales rather than representing a common characteristic of all rainfall records (Endo et al., 2009; Dinpashoh et al., 2014).
[75] The greater persistence observed in the annual and wet-season series may reflect the cumulative influence of interannual climate variability on seasonal rainfall accumulation. However, because atmospheric circulation was not examined directly, this interpretation should be regarded as a plausible physical explanation rather than direct evidence of climate forcing. The observed heterogeneity in autocorrelation supports the conditional application of TFPW, ensuring that pre-whitening was applied only to rainfall series exhibiting significant serial dependence before trend detection (Blain, 2014).

[79] 3.2 Overall pattern of annual and seasonal rainfall trends
[80] The conditional TFPW-MK analysis showed that rainfall change during 1981–2014 was expressed more clearly at the seasonal than at the annual timescale. Of the 36 station–timescale series analysed, seven (19.4%) exhibited statistically significant monotonic trends at the 5% significance level, comprising one annual increase, three wet-season decreases, and three dry-season increases. The remaining 29 series showed no significant monotonic trends, although both positive and negative Sen's slope estimates were observed (Figure 2).
[81] Seasonal analyses revealed a more coherent pattern than annual rainfall alone. Station 500006 showed a significant decrease in wet-season rainfall together with a significant increase in dry-season rainfall, while annual rainfall remained statistically unchanged. This contrasting seasonal behaviour indicates that annual rainfall stability can conceal important changes in the seasonal distribution of rainfall. Consistent with this finding, all significant seasonal trends occurred as either wet-season decreases or dry-season increases, with no significant wet-season increases detected.
[82] These results suggest that rainfall change in Prachuap Khiri Khan was characterised primarily by shifts in seasonal rainfall distribution rather than by widespread changes in annual rainfall totals. This pattern should not be interpreted as a direct transfer of rainfall between seasons but instead as increasing differentiation in rainfall occurrence throughout the year while annual accumulation remained relatively stable.
[83] From a water-resources perspective, seasonal rainfall changes may have greater practical importance than changes in annual totals because reservoir recharge, irrigation scheduling, soil-moisture replenishment, and water availability depend strongly on the timing of rainfall (Asfaw et al., 2018). These findings demonstrate that seasonal analysis provides a more informative assessment of hydroclimatic change than annual rainfall totals alone in coastal western Thailand.

[87] 3.3 Annual rainfall trends
[88] Annual rainfall remained largely stable across Prachuap Khiri Khan Province during 1981–2014 (Table 2; Figure 2). Of the 12 monitoring stations, only station 500002 exhibited a statistically significant increasing trend, with a Sen's slope of +9.18 mm yr⁻¹ (95% CI: +0.99 to +16.15 mm yr⁻¹; p = 0.024). The remaining stations showed both positive and negative Sen's slope estimates, but none were statistically significant because their 95% confidence intervals included zero.
[89] The predominance of non-significant annual trends suggests that total annual rainfall changed little over the study period. However, annual rainfall represents the cumulative effect of precipitation across different seasons and may therefore mask contrasting seasonal changes occurring within the same year. Consequently, annual rainfall alone provides limited information for identifying changes in rainfall timing and seasonal distribution (Pascale et al., 2015).
[90] The spatial variability of annual Sen's slope estimates indicates that rainfall responses differed among monitoring stations, reflecting the heterogeneous hydroclimatic characteristics of the province. Although annual rainfall appeared generally stable, the seasonal analyses presented in the following sections reveal changes that are not evident from annual rainfall totals alone.

[95] 3.4 Wet-season rainfall trends
[96] Wet-season rainfall exhibited clearer evidence of change than annual rainfall (Table 3; Figure 2). Significant decreasing trends were detected at stations 500003, 500005, and 500006, with Sen's slopes of −7.62, −5.61, and −6.29 mm yr⁻¹, respectively (95% confidence intervals excluding zero). All three series showed significant lag-1 autocorrelation and were therefore analysed using the conditional TFPW-MK procedure.
[97] The significant declines were confined to three stations, whereas the remaining stations exhibited weak positive or negative trends that were not statistically significant. This spatial variability indicates that changes in wet-season rainfall were localized rather than uniform across the province. Such heterogeneity is consistent with the diverse physiographic characteristics of Prachuap Khiri Khan, where coastal proximity and the Tenasserim Range may influence local rainfall patterns over relatively short distances (Khalil et al., 2018). However, these factors were not examined directly in the present study and should therefore be regarded as plausible physical interpretations rather than confirmed causal mechanisms.
[98] From a hydrological perspective, declining wet-season rainfall is particularly important because the southwest monsoon supplies most of the annual water input to the province. Persistent reductions during this season may influence reservoir recharge, soil-moisture replenishment, and water availability for agriculture. Although the magnitude of these impacts cannot be inferred from rainfall trends alone, the identified stations represent locations where changes in seasonal rainfall warrant continued monitoring and consideration in water-resource planning.

[103] 3.5 Dry-season rainfall trends
[104] Dry-season rainfall exhibited predominantly increasing trends across the monitoring network (Table 4; Figure 2). Statistically significant increases were detected at stations 500002, 500004, and 500006, with Sen's slopes of +5.77, +5.22, and +6.78 mm yr⁻¹, respectively (95% confidence intervals excluding zero). No station exhibited a statistically significant decreasing trend.
[105] Although most of the remaining stations also showed positive Sen's slope estimates, their 95% confidence intervals encompassed zero, indicating that the observed increases could not be distinguished from natural interannual variability with statistical confidence. Nevertheless, the predominance of positive slopes suggests an overall tendency toward increasing dry-season rainfall during the study period.
[106] Unlike the annual and wet-season rainfall series, none of the dry-season series exhibited significant lag-1 autocorrelation. Accordingly, dry-season trends were evaluated using the original Mann–Kendall test without pre-whitening, suggesting that serial persistence had a negligible influence on trend detection during the dry season.
[107] From a water-resources perspective, increasing dry-season rainfall may contribute to improved soil-moisture availability and reduced irrigation demand in some areas. However, these increases should not be interpreted as compensating for declining wet-season rainfall because seasonal water availability depends on both the timing and magnitude of precipitation. Together with the wet-season results, these findings indicate that rainfall change in Prachuap Khiri Khan was characterised primarily by contrasting seasonal behaviour rather than by uniform changes in annual rainfall.

[112] 3.6 Evidence of seasonal rainfall redistribution
[113] The most important finding of this study is that rainfall change in Prachuap Khiri Khan Province was expressed more clearly through seasonal variation than through annual rainfall totals. While only one station exhibited a significant annual trend, six significant trends were identified in the seasonal analyses, comprising three wet-season decreases and three dry-season increases. This contrast indicates that annual rainfall alone may underestimate hydroclimatically important changes occurring within individual seasons.
[114] Station 500006 provides the clearest evidence of this pattern. Wet-season rainfall decreased significantly (−6.29 mm yr⁻¹), whereas dry-season rainfall increased significantly (+6.78 mm yr⁻¹), despite the absence of a significant annual trend (Figure 3). These opposing seasonal trends substantially offset one another when aggregated annually, demonstrating how stable annual rainfall can conceal significant changes in seasonal rainfall behaviour. The term seasonal rainfall redistribution is therefore used to describe contrasting long-term changes in seasonal rainfall while annual totals remain comparatively stable, rather than implying a direct transfer of rainfall from one season to another.
[115] These findings highlight the importance of examining rainfall at the seasonal rather than solely the annual scale. Changes in rainfall timing can influence reservoir recharge, irrigation scheduling, soil-moisture replenishment, and seasonal water availability even where annual rainfall exhibits little long-term change (Azam et al., 2018). Integrating seasonal and annual assessments therefore provides a more comprehensive understanding of hydroclimatic variability and better informs water-resource planning and climate adaptation in coastal western Thailand.

[117] 3.7 Influence of TFPW on statistical inference
[118] Comparison between the original Mann–Kendall (MK) test and the conditional TFPW–MK procedure (Table 5) indicates that the seven significant trends can be grouped according to the influence of serial correlation. Four series (500002 annual, 500002 dry, 500004 dry, and 500006 dry) showed no significant lag-1 autocorrelation; consequently, pre-whitening was unnecessary and both methods produced identical results. Two autocorrelated wet-season series (500005 and 500006) remained significant after pre-whitening, indicating that the detected trends were robust to autocorrelation correction. Only one series (500003 wet) changed from non-significant under the original MK test (p = 0.062) to significant after TFPW–MK (p = 0.034), suggesting that its statistical significance depends on the treatment of serial correlation. Accordingly, this result should be interpreted with caution, whereas the contrasting seasonal pattern at station 500006 is supported by both methods and therefore provides the strongest evidence for seasonal rainfall redistribution.

[134] 4. Conclusions
[135] This study showed that rainfall change in Prachuap Khiri Khan Province during 1981–2014 was expressed more clearly through seasonal variation than through changes in annual rainfall totals. Annual rainfall remained largely stable across the province, whereas significant wet-season decreases and dry-season increases revealed contrasting seasonal behaviour at several stations. These findings demonstrate that annual rainfall alone may underestimate hydroclimatically important changes in rainfall timing and seasonal distribution (Jiang et al., 2025; Suo et al., 2025).
[136] The clearest evidence of this pattern was observed at station 500006, where significant wet-season and dry-season trends occurred in opposite directions despite the absence of a significant annual trend. This result highlights that seasonal rainfall analysis provides substantially greater insight into hydroclimatic change than annual assessments alone and supports the concept of seasonal rainfall redistribution in coastal western Thailand.
[137] Comparison of the original Mann–Kendall and conditional TFPW-MK procedures showed that the treatment of serial correlation affected the inference for only one series (500003 wet); the other significant trends were either free of significant autocorrelation or remained significant after pre-whitening. These results support a conditional TFPW framework in which pre-whitening is applied only to series exhibiting significant lag-1 autocorrelation.
[138] From a water-resources perspective, the findings emphasize the importance of season-specific and station-specific rainfall assessments (Xie et al., 2025) for irrigation planning, reservoir operation, and climate-change adaptation. Future studies should extend the observational record, incorporate denser monitoring networks, and investigate the climatic drivers responsible for the observed seasonal rainfall changes.
